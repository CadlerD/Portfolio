About

An undergraduate research project on where AI's energy cost actually comes from

Most conversations about AI energy consumption point to hardware: bigger chips, bigger datacenters, more efficient cooling. This project asks a different question — how much of that cost is determined earlier, by the structural quality of the inputs a model is asked to process, and by the design choices developers make around them.

Research

The Structural–Computational Efficiency Framework

SCEF argues that AI energy consumption is a downstream consequence of input data structural quality and developer design choices — not solely a hardware problem. Poorly structured, ambiguous, or redundant prompts force models to draw on more processing power to resolve them into a coherent response, which means linguistic inefficiency becomes energy inefficiency.

The common framing

Energy consumption is treated as a fixed cost of scale — solved by better chips, denser datacenters, and greener power grids. The prompt itself is assumed to be a neutral input.

SCEF's framing

The prompt is not neutral. Its readability, lexical redundancy, and hedging carry a measurable downstream cost — one that structural design choices, not just hardware, can reduce.

Instrument

Telio: testing SCEF empirically

To move SCEF from theory to evidence, this project built Telio — a Python research instrument that scores a prompt's linguistic quality, sends it to the Claude API, logs the real token usage that comes back, and converts that into an estimated energy cost using a configurable conversion constant.
